HIPAA, CLAS, and Language Access: What Healthcare Providers Need to Know

October 9, 2025·interpretingWorks

Language access isn’t optional in healthcare — it’s a federal requirement. Yet many providers still rely on ad-hoc solutions like bilingual staff, family members, or unqualified interpreters. These approaches carry serious legal and clinical risk.

The Legal Framework

Three federal laws create overlapping obligations for healthcare organizations:

Title VI of the Civil Rights Act (1964) requires any organization receiving federal funding to provide meaningful access to individuals with limited English proficiency (LEP). Hospitals, clinics, and federally qualified health centers all fall under this obligation.

Section 1557 of the ACA reinforces Title VI and explicitly prohibits discrimination on the basis of national origin — which includes language — in any health program receiving federal financial assistance.

The CLAS Standards (Culturally and Linguistically Appropriate Services) from HHS provide a framework for delivering equitable, culturally responsive care, including language access services.

What “Meaningful Access” Means

Meaningful access means a patient can communicate effectively with their provider — not just exchange basic information. For an LEP patient, that requires a qualified medical interpreter, not a bilingual receptionist who happens to speak the language.

A qualified medical interpreter:

  • Has completed formal interpreter training
  • Understands medical terminology in both languages
  • Adheres to a professional code of ethics (impartiality, confidentiality, accuracy)
  • Is credentialed and background-checked

The Risk of Using Untrained Interpreters

Clinical errors due to miscommunication are well documented. Using a patient’s family member as an interpreter — especially a minor child — is discouraged by professional guidelines and can create liability. If a patient is harmed because critical information was lost in translation, the provider may be held responsible.
